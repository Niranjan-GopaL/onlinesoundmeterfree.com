If you have ever looked at a sound level reading and thought "85 and 88 dB are basically the same," this article is for you. On a decibel meter, a 3 dB jump looks tiny. In reality it represents a doubling of sound energy — and it can cut the time you can safely listen in half.

## Decibels don't add up the way you expect

The decibel scale is logarithmic. That means it measures ratios, not absolute steps. Every 10 dB increase is roughly ten times more sound intensity and about twice as loud to your ears. A consequence of this maths is that **a 3 dB increase doubles the sound energy** reaching your eardrum.

This is also why you cannot simply add decibels together. Two identical machines each producing 80 dB do not make 160 dB — they make about 83 dB. Three of them make about 85 dB. The numbers grow slowly on the display while the energy stacks up quickly.

## The exchange rate

Hearing damage depends on two things: how loud a sound is, and how long you are exposed to it. NIOSH combines these into a single rule using what is called an **exchange rate** of 3 dB.

The recommended exposure limit is 85 dBA averaged over 8 hours. For every 3 dB above that, the safe duration halves:

- 85 dBA — 8 hours
- 88 dBA — 4 hours
- 91 dBA — 2 hours
- 94 dBA — 1 hour
- 97 dBA — 30 minutes
- 100 dBA — 15 minutes

By 100 dBA — a loud nightclub or a handheld drill — you reach your entire daily safe dose in a quarter of an hour.

## Why it matters in everyday life

Most people never measure their noise exposure, so the risk is invisible until hearing loss appears years later. A few practical takeaways:

- **Concerts and clubs** routinely hit 100–110 dBA. A single night can exceed a week's safe dose. Earplugs that cut 15–20 dB make an enormous difference.
- **Power tools and lawn equipment** often sit at 90–100 dBA. Hearing protection turns an unsafe afternoon into a safe one.
- **Headphones** can easily exceed 100 dBA at high volume. The "60/60 rule" — no more than 60% volume for 60 minutes — keeps you in a safer range.

Noise is one of the most underestimated factors in a classroom. Too much of it makes speech hard to follow, raises everyone's stress, and quietly erodes concentration — and children are more affected than adults. A simple [sound meter projected on the board](/sound-meter-for-classroom/) turns an abstract "be quieter" into something students can see and manage themselves.

## What the research suggests

The World Health Organization recommends a background noise level of around **35 dBA** in classrooms for clear teaching. Above that, the signal-to-noise ratio between a teacher's voice and the room drops, and comprehension suffers — especially for younger children, students learning in a second language, and anyone with hearing differences.

That 35 dBA figure is a *background* target — the room when it is supposed to be quiet. Active learning is naturally louder, and that's fine. The goal isn't silence; it's matching the noise to the activity.

## Target ranges by activity

These are practical guides, not rules. Use a meter to find what works in your space:

- **Independent or test work** — below 50 dB. Near-silence; only the sound of pencils and pages.
- **Partner talk** — 50–60 dB. Quiet conversation between two students.
- **Group work** — 60–68 dB. Several conversations at a comfortable level.
- **Whole-class discussion** — 60–70 dB, with one voice at a time.
- **Unfocused / too loud** — above 70 dB. A signal to pause and reset.

## Making it work in practice

A meter only helps if students buy into it. A few approaches teachers find effective:

- **Co-create the targets.** Agree the acceptable level for each activity with your class so it feels like a shared expectation, not a punishment.
- **Make it visual, not verbal.** Let the colour-coded gauge do the reminding. Pointing silently at a rising number is calmer and more effective than raising your voice over the room.
- **Celebrate the average.** Use the Avg or LAeq figure at the end of a session to recognise a genuinely focused lesson. It rewards the whole class, not just the quietest students.
- **Use it for transitions.** Clean-up and line-up are natural noise spikes. A glance at the meter helps students bring themselves back down.
